How the forecast is made

Weather drives electricity demand: cold mornings and hot afternoons push it up. This system turns an ensemble of weather forecasts into a range of possible demand for each grid area, then blends several models so the range is as honest as we can make it.

In plain language

  1. Start from the weather. Each day we take the ECMWF IFS ensemble, a set of slightly different weather forecasts that together show how uncertain the weather is. Temperatures and other fields are averaged over each grid area, weighted towards where people live.
  2. Turn weather into demand, four ways. A physics-based chain links temperature to heating and cooling load and corrects it with gradient boosting. A LightGBM model predicts demand percentiles directly. A neural network predicts a whole distribution. Chronos-2, a time-series foundation model we fine-tuned on demand, adds a view built from demand history.
  3. Blend them. A stacked mixture combines the four, weighting each by how well it did on years it never saw in training.
  4. Publish ranges and chances. For every area and day we publish 19 percentiles (5th to 95th), each area's top-10%, top-5% and top-1% levels for the time of year, and the chance of exceeding each level.

What goes in

  • Weather: ECMWF IFS open-data ensemble (medium range) for live runs; Copernicus ERA5 and ERA5T reanalysis for training.
  • Demand: U.S. EIA-930 hourly demand by balancing authority, plus FERC Form 714 for the longer training record.
  • Climate context: NOAA nClimGrid temperatures and NOAA CPC climate indices.
  • Calendar: day of week, holidays and season.

What comes out

  • Peak demand (MW): the highest hourly demand of each day, for days 1 to 14 after the issue date, and the highest day of weeks 1 and 2.
  • Daily energy (MWh per day; shown on this site in GWh): the total demand of each day, and of each week.
  • Days are local standard time for each area. Day 1 is the day after the forecast is issued; week 1 is days 1 to 7.
  • 29 balancing authorities that pass all of our data-quality checks (tier A).

Known limits

  • Heat waves: in research tests the full system showed no gain over the simpler D2 baseline during heat waves.
  • Interim mode is untested live: daily runs use the medium-range ensemble only. This mode has not yet been tested in live use, and some inputs (dewpoint and wind) are gap-filled.
  • Weeks 3 to 6 need extended-range weather data, which live runs do not have yet. Replays show them, but their research skill is close to climatology.
  • No live demand feed for two areas: PowerSouth (AEC) and Sierra Pacific (SPPC) have no live EIA-930 demand, so their live forecasts are flagged as not reliable and their chances of high demand are hidden.
  • Top-10% levels come from older history: each area's top-10%, top-5% and top-1% levels are set from past years. Where demand has since shifted upward, such as California this autumn, days above the level are more common than 1 in 10, so a high chance can be real. Compare with recent observed peaks before reading it as an error.
  • Megawatts are on a FERC Form 714 basis: live EIA-930 demand is scaled to match the FERC record the model was trained on. The scaling is small for most areas but large for Nevada Power (NEVP, about 0.66), Sacramento (BANC, about 0.63) and Duke Florida (FPC, about 0.74), so their megawatts sit below the EIA-930 figures. Percentages are not affected.
  • Energy forecasts have ranges, but no top-10% levels or exceedance chances yet.
  • Not for operations: this is a research product. Grid operators' own forecasts should drive dispatch.
